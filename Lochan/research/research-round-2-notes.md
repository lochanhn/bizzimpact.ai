# Small business problems and existing AI adoption

Research batch 2 · November 6, 2026 · bizzimpact.ai

This batch adds 17 downloaded publications and inspection of six GitHub projects. It investigates operational problems, current AI use, adoption barriers, and existing implementations. It does not finalize our product, questionnaire, or technology stack. The previous 51-question bank remains an unvalidated draft, not a form we expect every owner to complete.

All publication IDs, original links, local PDFs, versions, review depths and repository snapshots are in [research-round-2-sources.md](research-round-2-sources.md). The earlier evidence remains in [research-notes.md](research-notes.md).

## What this research changes

Three working conclusions deserve further product consideration:

1. **The starting point is often an existing workflow and existing software.** A business may already own relevant capabilities but lack the knowledge, configuration or time to use them. Discovery should investigate this before proposing another subscription.
2. **The task matters more than a broad industry label.** Drafting a message, capturing an enquiry, committing a booking and forecasting stock require different data, permissions, connections and levels of responsibility.
3. **A useful assessment should gather information when it changes a decision.** We should later reduce and sequence questions around actual constraints rather than ask owners to complete our entire research framework.

These are our interpretations of the evidence, not experimentally validated design rules. Detailed source notes follow.

## 1. Findings from the downloaded publications

### R2-P01 — OECD G7 adoption paper

The paper distinguishes adoption pathways by breadth and complexity rather than treating every SME as equally ready. Its coffee-roaster example uses ChatGPT for descriptions, SEO, emails and shipping-cost analysis. Its French bakery case combines existing commerce/advertising tools with AI assistance for responses, content and visuals. These are reported cases, not controlled demonstrations of profitability.

**Implication:** Recommending a custom AI system to an owner who needs occasional writing help may create needless effort. A workflow with several connected tools is a different adoption problem.

### R2-P02 — OECD 2025 digitalisation survey

The surveyed platform-connected businesses obtain skills through informal research and peers as well as training. Needs vary by business size, sector and digital maturity. Digital security is also uneven.

**Implication:** Setup help needs to match the person doing it. An owner without technical support may benefit from a familiar hosted feature rather than maintaining another application.

**Limit:** This sample is digitally selected and not representative; its adoption figures cannot describe all US microbusinesses.

### R2-P03 — OECD experimental-evidence review

The reviewed evidence finds benefits in some bounded tasks, but effects depend on the task, user expertise and how AI is incorporated. It does not establish universal gains from AI access. Evidence for long-term firm outcomes is thinner than evidence for particular tasks.

**Implication:** Describe the work a tool assists and its conditions. Do not translate a writing experiment into a promise of improved restaurant profit.

**Limit:** This review overlaps underlying studies elsewhere in our library; it is not another independent experiment confirming each result.

### R2-P04 — Census 2026 diffusion study

Firm-level adoption, use within business functions and worker-level tasks are separate measurements. An employee can use AI without formal adoption, while organizational deployment may occur outside employees' direct interactions. Reported adoption commonly covers a limited set of functions/tasks.

**Implication:** “Do you use AI?” alone tells us little about an owner's needs. Future assessment should focus on what happens in the relevant workflow.

**Limit:** Business-performance correlations do not prove AI caused growth, and employer reporting may undercount informal employee use.

### R2-P05 — Census 2024 tracking study

Historical AI applications include marketing automation, virtual agents and text/data analytics. Adoption can involve new workflows, staff training and cloud services. Some users expected to discontinue use, illustrating experimentation rather than permanent adoption.

**Implication:** A tool's availability is only one part of a workable change. The time and dependencies required to use it belong in the adoption assessment.

**Limit:** These are historical estimates with narrower production-related wording, not a directly comparable baseline for later broad AI-use surveys.

### R2-P06 — Generative AI at Work

The downloaded revision studies AI assistance for customer-support agents. Its preferred estimate reports roughly 14% more issues resolved per hour, with larger benefits for less experienced workers. This is assistance within an existing support workflow, not evidence for replacing a business's entire customer service operation.

**Implication:** Relevant capabilities may improve how existing staff work. The outcome should match the task—such as correctly resolved enquiries—rather than simply counting generated replies.

**Limit:** A large support operation differs substantially from a small café or field-service company; its effect size is not our expected benefit.

### R2-P07 — Local entrepreneur workshops

The study describes practical marketing and branding activities with local entrepreneurs, including mobile image workflows, website/SEO work and drafting assistance. Supportive demonstrations and help applying tools mattered alongside basic exposure. Participants also expressed reasons to avoid or limit use.

**Implication:** A sample task grounded in the owner's business can be more useful than a generic tutorial or unexplained product list. Comfortable non-use should remain an option.

**Limit:** A qualitative community study does not establish typical financial gains or prove an online-only guide can reproduce in-person support.

### R2-P08 — Industrial-SME systematic review

The review identifies knowledge gaps, costs and digital maturity among recurring implementation barriers, with 27 challenges overall. It also highlights poor description of starting conditions and limited ROI assessment in the reviewed case studies.

**Implication:** Record prerequisites and starting systems when evaluating tools. A technically successful case is difficult to transfer if another business lacks its data or support.

**Limit:** The review focuses on industrial SMEs and searched literature in 2022. It cannot establish current demand for generative-AI tools in cafés or service trades.

### R2-P09 — OECD 2026 digitalisation survey

Off-the-shelf, relatively isolated AI use remains common among adopters in this sample. Maintenance cost, training time and skills gaps persist even as adoption grows. The methodological section identifies both nonrandom recruitment and uneven country samples.

**Implication:** Knowing about tools is different from being able to maintain useful operations around them. A recommendation should make ongoing ownership clear.

**Limit:** The report's headline figures are not national US estimates. Its country-average method also differs from pooling every respondent equally.

### R2-P10 — US Chamber technology report

Small-business technology use involves multiple platforms. The employee-readiness section distinguishes providing tools and hoping staff learn them from offering on-the-job training.

**Implication:** A new AI product can add another place to manage information. Existing software and realistic learning support deserve attention before adding a subscription.

**Limit:** This is an association-sponsored online survey with self-reported results. It does not establish that adding more platforms causes better performance.

### R2-P11 — TouchBistro restaurant report

Operators report automating ordering, invoicing and marketing. Among non-automators, reliability, POS integration and upfront cost appear as barriers. The respondent profile is weighted toward established and multi-location operations rather than very small single-location restaurants.

**Implication:** Restaurant discovery should distinguish ordinary automation from AI and check the exact POS/setup involved. A tool can address the right task while still being a poor operational fit.

**Limit:** Vendor survey, 2024 fieldwork under a 2025 title; not proof of current AI use across mom-and-pop restaurants.

### R2-P12 — Kenyan/Nigerian SMB workplace interviews

Participants use AI for initial drafts, outreach, structured documents and other work, then revise, compare, verify or localize outputs. Tool switching and paid-tier choices occur within task-specific constraints. Publication is recent, but fieldwork took place in 2023.

**Implication:** First-draft speed is not the whole workload. Instructions should account for correction, appropriate language/context, and what must remain human-controlled.

**Limit:** Already-using, digitally connected participants; situated qualitative evidence, not a representative adoption survey or a current model benchmark.

### R2-P13 — Entrepreneurship integrative review

The framework examines how AI can expand capability while introducing trade-offs involving overconfidence, reduced engagement or unreliable information. It separates direct entrepreneurial evidence from studies of adjacent activities.

**Implication:** More generated analysis is not automatically better guidance. An owner needs a manageable decision with visible assumptions, rather than an expanding pile of AI suggestions.

**Limit:** A literature synthesis and conceptual framework, not a trial of our platform or an established questionnaire-length rule.

### R2-P14 — Rural Philippine SME study

The small exploratory study reports adaptations in inquiry handling and customer communication alongside literacy and connectivity constraints. It describes businesses maintaining non-digital alternatives when internet access fails.

**Implication:** Whether a workflow depends on reliable connectivity can change which options are feasible.

**Caution:** Although the paper states an AI-versus-automation distinction, some indicators include keyword replies and automated templates. That makes the contribution of AI itself unclear. Cross-sectional self-reports and a purposive sample cannot establish causal gains.

### R2-P15 — Danish labor-market study

The March 2026 revision has a new title and updated results. It finds changes in task organization and AI-related work without a detectable average effect on recorded earnings/hours over the studied early period.

**Implication:** Reported productivity, released capacity, payroll reductions and business profit are different outcomes. A time-saving estimate must not be presented as guaranteed cash savings.

**Limit:** Exposed Danish occupations and administrative labor outcomes differ from our target users. Earlier title/version summaries must not be mixed with this revision.

### R2-P16 — ServiceTitan trades report

The sample includes HVAC and commercial landscaping. Training, integration and understanding value appear as barriers; the report also describes using AI embedded in existing software. Named HVAC customer stories illustrate possible deployments, but are selected vendor accounts.

**Implication:** Before choosing another application, examine whether the current system already supplies the required capability. Marketing success examples need separate compatibility and cost checks.

**Limit:** Commercial, explicitly nonrepresentative survey. Subgroup denominators differ; reported customer improvements do not isolate AI's causal contribution.

### R2-P17 — Housecall Pro AI Advantage

The report describes enquiry capture at NW Moss Removal and reporting workflows at DeLong & Sons HVAC and Acadiana Comfort. Its use-case chart includes people who have tried AI as well as active users. Respondents are customers/industry contacts, not a population sample.

**Implication:** Administrative reporting deserves consideration alongside missed calls: an owner may need help interpreting an existing export rather than deploying an autonomous agent.

**Limit:** Vendor-selected accounts and self-reports; no independently verified savings. The failed 2025 PDF was replaced by this clearly identified 2026 report.

## 3. Existing GitHub implementations

All six were inspected as source/documentation. None was run. Details and commit-pinned links are in the companion source file. “Available code” is different from a supported, affordable product that an owner can deploy unaided.

### R2-G01 — Dify

**Observed:** A platform for AI applications, workflows and retrieval over documents. The selected API controller handles structured app inputs, queries and response modes.

**Relevance:** Illustrates how a business-facing knowledge assistant could be exposed through an API/UI.

**Limits:** It still needs content, model credentials, configuration and operation. The root license adds conditions to Apache 2.0, including multi-tenant and branding provisions. We have not chosen it as a dependency or checked commercial plans.

### R2-G02 — Flowise

**Observed:** A visual AI application builder with conversation-chain code and deployment documentation. At the inspected snapshot, GitHub marks it archived and the README explicitly says it has been archived.

**Relevance:** An implementation reference for configurable conversation components.

**Limits:** It should not be treated as an actively maintained recommendation merely because historical tutorials exist. The license distinguishes the community code from commercial areas. No deployment was attempted.

### R2-G03 — Activepieces

**Observed:** Workflow automation with separate integration “pieces.” The inspected Google Sheets registration exposes record actions/triggers; the OpenAI registration includes prompting, classification and structured-data actions. Documentation describes approval and human-input steps.

**Relevance:** Shows an existing route from AI output to familiar business records without writing every connector ourselves.

**Limits:** Registered actions do not prove a complete workflow works for an owner's account. Credentials, plan limits, data mappings and failures still matter. Community and enterprise licensing differ.

### R2-G04 — n8n

**Observed:** Workflow automation with an implemented OpenRouter model node. The selected source requires a credential and handles a response-format edge case.

**Relevance:** An existing connector mechanism could support future AI-to-software workflows. Provider integration is available code, not something we necessarily need to invent.

**Limits:** A connector alone does not deliver a business solution. n8n calls itself fair-code/source-available and uses Sustainable Use/enterprise terms; do not casually label all code permissive open source. No workflow was tested and no key was supplied.

### R2-G05 — Chatwoot

**Observed:** A customer-support application with multiple channels and advertised Captain AI. The selected Captain runner includes response handling, handoff state and error capture.

**Relevance:** Combining an inbox, AI assistance and human operators is an existing application pattern.

**Limits:** The inspected Captain source resides under `enterprise/`; the root license treats this differently from MIT community code. We cannot infer that every advertised AI feature is free or included in every self-hosted deployment.

### R2-G06 — Twilio speech-assistant sample

**Observed:** A Node/Fastify example bridges Twilio voice streams and an AI realtime connection. It requires external accounts/credentials and a reachable server endpoint.

**Relevance:** Concrete implementation evidence for the audio-conversation part of a possible receptionist workflow.

**Limits:** The inspected sample does not implement booking, calendar coordination, or a business database. Talking over a phone is not equivalent to completing an appointment workflow. No phone call was made; setup and usage costs were not estimated. Root license is MIT.

### What this comparison tells us

| Existing pattern | Examples inspected | Work still needed to serve an owner |
|---|---|---|
| AI over business knowledge | Dify, Flowise | Correct content, ownership, configuration, support and an appropriate maintenance choice |
| Connect AI to existing records | Activepieces, n8n | Exact connector support, permissions, field mapping and a manageable workflow |
| AI with human customer support | Chatwoot | Channel setup, feature availability, escalation and operating procedures |
| Voice interaction | Twilio example | Business rules, booking systems, transfer, reliability and ongoing operation |

This confirms existing building blocks, not real-world adoption by the businesses in our papers. We have not found a verified direct code-to-business link between these repositories and those specific reported cases.

**Jobber (R2-W01)** adds home-service survey context for quoting, invoicing, communication and capacity. Its high-confidence cohorts are selected by reported performance, so their AI use cannot prove AI caused that performance. This is vendor evidence, not a population benchmark we should automatically use for every trade.

## 5. Problems we can now investigate more precisely

| Problem family | What the evidence suggests | What we still need to distinguish |
|---|---|---|
| Enquiries and follow-up | Work can arrive when people cannot respond; existing intake solutions are reported | Capture versus answer versus commit a booking; channel and handoff requirements |
| Reporting and paperwork | AI is used around existing business records, drafts and administrative work | Available data, export quality, correctness and who makes the decision |
| Marketing and product content | Direct small-business examples and community research exist | Drafting versus publishing, brand fit, and whether demand is actually the bottleneck |
| Restaurant operations | Costs, ordinary automation and system compatibility matter | Whether the task needs AI, usable history exists, and the POS supports it |
| Staff knowledge/training | Support and practical learning affect adoption | Helping staff perform work versus solving physical worker shortages |
| Technology adoption itself | Understanding fit, getting started, connections and maintenance remain issues | Unawareness versus unsuitable tools versus unaffordable or impractical implementation |

These families overlap. We should later select a clear workflow, not bundle every issue into one recommendation.

## 6. Evidence that challenges our earlier assumptions

- **More tool choices can increase the owner's burden.** Research on skills and integration, plus existing embedded products, suggests an owner may need a clear path through familiar software. A bigger catalog is not automatically more useful.
- **“Industry + budget” is insufficient, but collecting every conceivable detail is also unjustified.** The additional information should resolve a genuine difference in task, feasible connection, required data, or acceptable effort.
- **AI must not be credited for every automation.** Static replies, reminders, file transfers and dashboards may solve useful problems without AI. Some sources blur this boundary; our own product should make it clearer.
- **Task gains cannot be converted directly into cash or growth promises.** Evidence ranges from task measurements to qualitative reports and correlations. Preserve those distinctions when presenting benefits.
- **Existing implementations are not all equally usable.** Maintenance status, license boundaries, deployment burden and missing business logic can disqualify an attractive GitHub example.

These are our synthesis and engineering/product interpretations. They are not a validated scoring system or proof of willingness to use our platform.

## 7. What this means for revisiting the questionnaire later

We should retire the idea of a fixed 51-question owner session. Keep the current file as draft history until we deliberately replace it. This research does not establish a scientifically correct universal question count.

When we return to design, each proposed question should pass a practical test:

1. Which decision changes because of the answer?
2. Can we obtain it from an earlier explicit answer or a business description instead?
3. Is it necessary now, or only after the owner chooses a task/candidate?
4. Can an owner reasonably know the answer, and what happens if they do not?
5. Is the expected benefit of asking greater than the burden?

A promising direction is a reusable business description followed by a task-specific conversation that asks only for unresolved constraints. This is a proposal to evaluate later, not a revised final questionnaire or an instruction to implement a chatbot now.

## 8. What remains uncertain

- Which exact sector/task should be our first demonstration and why it wins over alternatives.
- Whether independent, current evidence establishes unmet need after existing software features are considered.
- Whether our discovery experience would improve an owner's decision compared with direct search or their vendor's help.
- Current prices, plan restrictions and actual compatibility for a selected shortlist.
- Measured owner completion burden, comprehension and willingness to use the product. Desk research does not provide these measurements.
- How much transfer is justified from international SMEs, industrial firms and large customer-support operations to US microbusinesses.
